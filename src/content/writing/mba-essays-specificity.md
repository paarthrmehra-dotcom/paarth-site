The typical rejected essay is well written, well structured, and interchangeable with two thousand others. It describes leading a team through a difficult project, learning the importance of listening, and wanting to scale that impact.

None of it is untrue. All of it is unusable, because anyone in your industry could have written it.

## Specificity is the whole game

A committee is building a class, which means looking for people who bring something the rest of the room does not. That thing is almost never a title or a metric. It is a perspective formed by a particular set of decisions.

So the useful question is not "what was my biggest achievement" but "what do I believe about my industry that most people in it do not, and what happened that made me believe it." The second question produces an essay only you can write.

## Show the decision, not the outcome

Outcomes are cheap to claim and impossible to verify. Decisions reveal judgement. An essay that spends its middle on a genuinely hard choice — what you gave up, what you were wrong about, what you would do differently — earns more credibility than one listing results.

The instinct to hide the wrong turn is strong and should be resisted. Describing your own error precisely signals exactly the self-awareness the class is selected for.

## "Why this school" has to cost you something

Naming three courses and a club is table stakes and reads as research rather than fit. A stronger answer explains a real trade-off you are making by choosing this school over the obvious alternative for someone with your profile, and why that trade-off makes sense given what you want to do afterwards.

## Start earlier than the deadline suggests

The positioning work — figuring out what the application is arguing — takes longer than the writing. Candidates who begin essays in September are usually still discovering their story in October, when they should be editing.
